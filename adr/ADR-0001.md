# ADR-0001: Platform Architecture Style

**Document**: Architecture Decision Record
**ID**: ADR-0001
**Date**: 2026-06-25
**Status**: Accepted
**Deciders**: Platform Architect, Core Engineering Team
**Constitution Reference**: Articles I, II, V, VI, XVII

---

## 1. Context

The Unified Healthcare Platform requires a foundational architecture that can operate across diverse environments—from isolated government laboratories with intermittent connectivity to national-scale deployments with full infrastructure. The platform must maintain data sovereignty, support offline-first operations, and comply with Iraqi government regulations while remaining technically extensible for future healthcare modules.

Platform-Core serves as the authoritative governance, runtime, intelligence, and integration platform for every repository within the ecosystem. All modules—from laboratory information systems to workforce management—must depend on Platform-Core for shared services, authentication, policy enforcement, and ecosystem coordination.

The Iraqi healthcare infrastructure presents unique constraints:
- Government laboratories may have limited or no internet connectivity
- Data must remain within national boundaries for sovereignty compliance
- Legacy systems require integration without disrupting existing operations
- Multi-site deployments must coordinate without centralized infrastructure
- Security requirements demand government-grade protection standards

The Constitution (Article I) establishes that the platform must be modular, extensible, observable, secure, AI-ready, event-driven, offline-first, cloud-ready, government-ready, and national-scale ready. These principles directly constrain architectural choices and eliminate options that cannot satisfy all requirements simultaneously.

---

## 2. Problem

The platform needs an architecture that simultaneously satisfies these competing requirements:

**Requirement 1 - Offline-First Operation**: The platform must function fully without internet connectivity. Government laboratories in remote provinces may lose network access for extended periods. Critical healthcare operations cannot depend on cloud availability.

**Requirement 2 - Module Independence**: Each repository must remain independent (Constitution Article II). Modules communicate through APIs, events, shared SDKs, and published contracts—never through shared databases or code duplication.

**Requirement 3 - Government-Grade Security**: Iraqi government healthcare data requires sovereignty controls, audit trails, encryption, and compliance with national security regulations. The architecture must support air-gapped deployments.

**Requirement 4 - National Scalability**: The platform must scale from single-laboratory deployments to national networks spanning multiple provinces without architectural redesign (Constitution Article XIV).

**Requirement 5 - Event-Driven Communication**: All significant business actions must publish events (Constitution Article VI). Events must be immutable, versioned, and documented.

**Requirement 6 - Healthcare Domain Requirements**: Medical device integration, laboratory workflows, patient data handling, and healthcare standard compliance (HL7, FHIR, LOINC) require specialized architectural considerations.

**Requirement 7 - AI Integration**: The platform must support AI-assisted operations while preventing autonomous production deployment (Constitution Article IX). AI recommendations require human verification.

**Requirement 8 - Continuous Evolution**: The platform must evolve continuously without architectural redesign (Constitution Article XVI). New capabilities must improve maintainability, interoperability, reduce duplication, and increase automation.

## 3. Alternatives Considered

### Alternative A: Monolithic Architecture

**Description**: Single deployable unit containing all platform services, registries, and business logic.

**Pros**:
- Simple deployment and operational model
- Low latency for in-process calls
- Easy debugging and testing
- Single database with ACID transactions
- Minimal infrastructure requirements

**Cons**:
- Violates Constitution Article II (module independence)
- Cannot scale individual components independently
- Single point of failure for entire platform
- Difficult to support offline-first with cloud-only deployment
- Technology lock-in (single language, framework)
- Cannot support multi-site deployments without complete replication
- Deployment of one feature requires redeployment of entire platform
- Testing complexity grows quadratically with codebase size

**Assessment**: REJECTED — Fundamentally incompatible with modular architecture principle and module independence requirement.

### Alternative B: Traditional Microservices (Network-Only)

**Description**: Independently deployable services communicating exclusively via network APIs (REST, gRPC).

**Pros**:
- Independent deployment and scaling
- Technology diversity per service
- Clear service boundaries
- Fault isolation between services
- Team autonomy per service

**Cons**:
- Network dependency violates offline-first requirement
- High operational complexity (service mesh, load balancing, service discovery)
- Distributed transactions difficult to implement
- Network latency impacts user experience
- Requires infrastructure (Kubernetes, service mesh) not available in all deployments
- Debugging distributed systems significantly harder
- Eventual consistency challenges for healthcare data

**Assessment**: REJECTED — Network dependency fundamentally incompatible with offline-first and government laboratory constraints.

### Alternative C: Event-Sourced CQRS with Full Infrastructure

**Description**: Command Query Responsibility Segregation with event sourcing, requiring Kafka/RabbitMQ, separate read/write databases, and full infrastructure stack.

**Pros**:
- Complete audit trail via event sourcing
- Optimized read and write models
- Strong event-driven capabilities
- Temporal queries and state reconstruction
- Scalable read and write paths

**Cons**:
- Excessive complexity for initial platform requirements
- Requires full infrastructure stack (message brokers, multiple databases)
- Eventual consistency may be inappropriate for some healthcare operations
- Steep learning curve for development team
- Operational overhead for government deployments
- Over-engineering for single-laboratory deployments

**Assessment**: REJECTED — Infrastructure requirements incompatible with offline-first and government deployment constraints. Complexity not justified by current requirements.

### Alternative D: Event-Driven Microservices with In-Process Runtime

**Description**: Modular microservice architecture using in-process event bus for runtime communication, with REST APIs for external communication, supporting both online and offline deployment modes.

**Pros**:
- Supports offline-first via in-process runtime (no network dependency)
- Module independence via API/event/SDK contracts
- Event-driven communication via in-process pub/sub
- Supports government-grade security (local-first data)
- Scalable from single-lab to national deployment
- Technology diversity via language-specific modules
- Supports AI integration with human oversight
- Continuous evolution without redesign

**Cons**:
- In-process event bus limited to single process (requires design consideration)
- Synchronization complexity for multi-site eventual consistency
- Requires careful design for state management
- More complex than monolithic architecture

**Assessment**: SELECTED — Best balance of requirements satisfaction. In-process runtime enables offline-first while event-driven design supports module independence and continuous evolution.

---

## 4. Decision

**We will adopt event-driven microservices with in-process runtime as the platform architecture style.**

### 4.1 Architecture Principles Implementation

| Principle | Implementation Strategy |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Modular | Each module is an independent repository with clear boundaries |
| Extensible | Plugin architecture via Platform-Core Plugin Engine |
| Observable | Structured logging, OpenTelemetry metrics, distributed tracing |
| Secure | JWT-based auth, RBAC, encryption, audit logging |
| AI-ready | AI recommendations tracked, confidence-scored, human-verified |
| Event-driven | In-process event bus with Redis Streams for production |
| Offline-first | Local SQLite databases with eventual synchronization |
| Cloud-ready | Docker containers, Kubernetes-compatible deployment |
| Government-ready | Audit trail, compliance reporting, data sovereignty |
| National-scale ready | Horizontal scaling, multi-tenancy architecture |

### 4.2 Communication Patterns

**In-Process Communication (Runtime)**:
- In-process event bus for component communication within Platform-Core
- Direct method calls for tightly-coupled internal operations
- Shared memory for high-performance data access

**Inter-Process Communication (Network)**:
- REST APIs for synchronous service-to-service calls
- GraphQL for flexible data queries
- WebSocket for real-time event streaming
- gRPC for high-performance internal communication (optional)

**Asynchronous Communication**:
- Redis Streams for production event bus
- Local event queue for offline operations
- Event replay for synchronization recovery

### 4.3 Deployment Modes

**Mode 1 - Standalone (Offline)**:
- SQLite for local data storage
- In-process event bus only
- No network dependencies
- Single-process deployment

**Mode 2 - Laboratory (LAN)**:
- PostgreSQL for shared laboratory data
- In-process event bus with optional Redis
- LAN-based API communication
- Docker Compose deployment

**Mode 3 - Cloud (Online)**:
- PostgreSQL with replicas for high availability
- Redis Streams for event bus
- Full API and event communication
- Kubernetes deployment

### 4.4 Data Management

Each module maintains its own database:
- SQLite for offline/edge deployments
- PostgreSQL for server/cloud deployments
- No shared databases between modules (Constitution Article III)
- Eventual synchronization via event-driven replication

### 4.5 Security Architecture

- JWT-based authentication for API access
- API key authentication for service-to-service
- OAuth2 for external integrations
- RBAC with roles: Platform Architect, Team Lead, Developer, Viewer
- Resource-level permissions for registry operations
- Policy-based access control for governance operations
- Encryption at rest and in transit
- No hardcoded secrets

## 5. Consequences

### 5.1 Positive Consequences

**Operational Benefits**:
- Platform functions without internet connectivity in government laboratories
- Modules can be developed, tested, and deployed independently
- Single-laboratory deployments are simple and lightweight
- National-scale deployments can be achieved by adding infrastructure
- Government security requirements are satisfied by local-first data

**Development Benefits**:
- Clear module boundaries reduce development complexity
- Event-driven design enables loose coupling between teams
- In-process runtime simplifies debugging and testing
- Plugin architecture allows extension without modification
- Language-specific modules can use optimal technology per domain

**Compliance Benefits**:
- Constitution Article II (module independence) is satisfied by API/event/SDK contracts
- Constitution Article III (no shared databases) is satisfied by per-module databases
- Constitution Article VI (event governance) is satisfied by event-driven communication
- Constitution Article XVII (prohibitions) is satisfied by enforced patterns

### 5.2 Negative Consequences

**Complexity Costs**:
- In-process event bus requires careful design for message routing
- Synchronization between offline and online modes adds operational complexity
- Multiple deployment modes increase testing matrix
- Eventual consistency requires conflict resolution strategies

**Performance Costs**:
- In-process event bus introduces serialization overhead
- Event-driven patterns add latency compared to direct method calls
- Synchronization operations consume bandwidth when connectivity is available
- Local databases may require periodic consolidation

**Operational Costs**:
- Multiple database technologies (SQLite, PostgreSQL) increase operational overhead
- Deployment mode selection requires documentation and training
- Monitoring distributed event flows is more complex than monolithic logging
- Backup and recovery strategies differ by deployment mode

## 6. Trade-offs

### 6.1 Consistency vs. Availability

**Trade-off**: The architecture prioritizes availability over strong consistency for offline operations.

**Rationale**: Healthcare operations in government laboratories cannot wait for network connectivity. Laboratories must be able to record results, manage samples, and generate reports even when disconnected. Strong consistency will be maintained within each deployment mode, with eventual consistency across modes.

**Mitigation**: Event sourcing with conflict resolution ensures data integrity during synchronization. Timestamp-based ordering and manual conflict resolution for critical data.

### 6.2 Simplicity vs. Flexibility

**Trade-off**: The architecture is more complex than a monolith but more flexible than traditional microservices.

**Rationale**: The diverse deployment requirements (single lab to national scale) justify the additional complexity. A monolith cannot satisfy offline-first and national-scale simultaneously.

**Mitigation**: Clear documentation, deployment guides, and operational runbooks reduce complexity burden. Platform-Core provides shared infrastructure to reduce complexity for individual modules.

### 6.3 Performance vs. Independence

**Trade-off**: In-process communication is faster but limits independent scaling.

**Rationale**: For initial deployment, in-process performance benefits outweigh scaling limitations. Network-based communication can be added for specific high-throughput scenarios.

**Mitigation**: Profiling identifies performance-critical paths. Critical paths can be optimized with direct method calls or dedicated communication channels.

### 6.4 Security vs. Usability

**Trade-off**: Government-grade security adds operational overhead for developers and users.

**Rationale**: Iraqi government regulations and healthcare data protection requirements mandate strong security. Security cannot be compromised for usability.

**Mitigation**: Developer experience improvements (SDK, documentation, tooling) reduce security burden. User experience optimization within security constraints.

## 7. Risks

### 7.1 Technical Risks

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Mitigation |
|------|-------------|--------|------------|
| In-process event bus becomes bottleneck | Medium | High | Monitor metrics, optimize routing, consider external bus for high-throughput |
| Synchronization conflicts cause data loss | Medium | High | Implement conflict detection, manual resolution UI, audit trail |
| SQLite limitations affect data integrity | Low | Medium | Regular backups, migration path to PostgreSQL, integrity checks |
| Offline mode creates security vulnerabilities | Low | High | Local encryption, credential caching, audit logging |

### 7.2 Operational Risks

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Mitigation |
|------|-------------|--------|------------|
| Deployment mode confusion | Medium | Medium | Clear documentation, deployment guides, validation checks |
| Monitoring gaps in offline mode | Medium | Medium | Local metrics collection, periodic sync for monitoring data |
| Backup complexity across modes | Low | Medium | Automated backup scripts per mode, recovery testing |
| Team expertise gaps | Medium | Medium | Training programs, documentation, code examples |

### 7.3 Compliance Risks

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Mitigation |
|------|-------------|--------|------------|
| Constitution violations undetected | Low | High | Automated governance checks, continuous compliance monitoring |
| Government audit failures | Low | High | Comprehensive audit trail, compliance reporting, regular audits |
| Data sovereignty violations | Low | High | Data residency controls, encryption, access logging |
